# Menopause Treatment: Hormone Therapy and Other Options

Hot flashes, night sweats and disrupted sleep can start during perimenopause, years before periods stop. Treatment choices include hormone therapy, nonhormonal prescription medicines and some behavioral approaches. The right choice depends on your symptoms, medical history and preferences. You can ask for help without waiting until symptoms become unbearable.

Symptoms can begin while periods are still occurring. You do not need to wait for a particular birthday or for periods to stop completely before asking for care. A treatment plan can focus on the symptom that matters most to you, whether that is interrupted sleep, hot flashes, vaginal discomfort or changes in mood.

Start with [hormone therapy questions](#hormones), consider [nonhormonal options](#nonhormonal), and bring a [short list to your appointment](#questions). A clinician can also help assess symptoms that may have another cause. No single treatment is right for everyone.

## What to discuss about hormone therapy

[ACOG describes systemic hormone therapy](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/hormone-therapy-for-menopause) as the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. Whether it is appropriate for you depends on factors such as your age, time since menopause, personal history and risks of blood clots, stroke, heart disease and certain cancers. Review these with an ob-gyn or another qualified clinician instead of using a single rule for everyone.

The type of hormone therapy matters. If you still have a uterus, systemic estrogen is generally paired with a progestin to lower the risk of uterine cancer. If you have had a hysterectomy, estrogen alone may be an option. ACOG also distinguishes systemic treatment from low-dose local vaginal estrogen for vaginal symptoms. Your clinician can explain which formulation and route fit your situation.

### Systemic versus local treatment

Systemic therapy circulates through the body and can be prescribed in different forms. Low-dose local vaginal treatment is used for specific vaginal symptoms and is a different discussion from treating hot flashes throughout the body. Ask what a proposed treatment is meant to help, how to use it and when to check whether it is working.

A personal decision includes more than choosing a product. Tell your clinician about your medical history, medicines, any history of blood clots or cancer, and whether you have a uterus. Your preferences about the route of treatment, follow-up and side effects matter too. If a therapy is not suitable, ask about alternatives rather than assuming there is nothing else to try.

### What about timing and duration?

Age and time since menopause are part of the benefit-and-risk discussion, but a date alone doesn't settle whether a treatment is right for an individual. Ask your clinician how those factors apply to you and how often to reassess the plan. Do not start, stop or alter prescribed hormones without discussing it with the prescriber.

If you have unexpected vaginal bleeding, tell a clinician promptly. Bleeding can have several causes; it should be assessed rather than automatically attributed to hormones or menopause.

## If hormones are not right for you

There are prescription options for hot flashes that do not contain estrogen, including certain antidepressants and newer neurokinin receptor antagonists. The choice depends on your symptoms, other medicines and health history. Ask which benefits, adverse effects, monitoring and follow-up apply before starting a medicine.

For vaginal dryness or pain with sex, ask specifically about local treatment options. These symptoms deserve care, even if you are not taking systemic hormone therapy.

### How to compare treatment choices

Ask what symptom each option targets. A medicine offered for hot flashes may have a different effect on sleep, mood or vaginal discomfort, and a medicine that suits one person's history may not suit another's. Your clinician can explain possible interactions with medicines you already take and any monitoring required.

You can ask: “What benefit should I expect, how soon should we check in, and what side effects should prompt a call?” If the first option doesn't help or causes troublesome effects, that is useful information for a follow-up visit; don't assume you have exhausted every choice.

## Can non-drug approaches help?

Behavioral approaches can be part of a treatment plan, especially when sleep and stress are affected. Ask your clinician which options have evidence for your particular symptoms and what to expect from them. They need not replace effective medical treatment if you want it.

## What about black cohosh and other supplements?

ACOG [does not recommend plant and herbal remedies for hot flashes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/experts-and-stories/ask-acog/what-can-i-do-to-help-with-hot-flashes), including black cohosh. Supplements can have side effects and interact with other medicines. Tell your clinician what you take, including products sold as “natural.” Avoid assuming that a supplement is proven or safe because it is widely sold.

### Make sleep and comfort part of the conversation

Night sweats can interrupt sleep, but they are not the only reason a person may wake at night. Note what you experience and whether snoring, pain, worry or another symptom is present. A clinician can help distinguish menopause symptoms from other contributors and discuss appropriate care.

Practical changes such as dressing in layers or keeping a room comfortable may make a hot flash easier to manage. They are supports, not guarantees and not substitutes for treatment when symptoms are disruptive. Movement and social connection can support general health without needing to be presented as a cure.

## Questions for your appointment

- Could my symptoms be related to perimenopause or menopause, or should we check for another cause?

- Am I a candidate for hormone therapy, and which type and route would fit my history?

- Which nonhormonal prescription options are suitable for me?

- What can help with sleep, mood or vaginal symptoms?

- How will we know whether treatment is working, and when should we review it?

Menopause care also includes prevention. Ask about bone health and which screenings are due. Our [heart-health guide](https://health.allwomenstalk.com/things-every-woman-needs-to-know-about-heart-disease/) can help you prepare questions about cardiovascular risk.

## How to think through the choice

There is no prize for choosing the most intervention or the least. Begin with the change you would most like to see: fewer night sweats, a more manageable workday, less discomfort during sex, or better sleep. Ask what evidence supports each option for that particular symptom. A plan may combine approaches, and it can change when your circumstances or preferences change.

If you have been told in the past that you cannot use hormones, ask which part of your history prompted that advice and whether it applies to every formulation or route. Equally, if you have been told hormones are suitable, ask how your personal history affects the decision. Neither question means you should override medical advice; both can make the reasoning clearer.

### Review possible side effects and monitoring

Before starting any prescription, ask which side effects are common, which are urgent, and who to contact if you have a concern. Discuss the other medicines and supplements you use, including over-the-counter products. A treatment may require a follow-up visit or particular monitoring; the schedule depends on the option and your health history.

If you experience an unexpected change after starting treatment, record when it began and call the prescribing clinician for advice. Do not assume every new symptom is a medication effect, and do not change the dose on your own. Seek urgent care for severe or sudden symptoms as advised by your care team.

### If your priority is vaginal comfort

Dryness, irritation, urinary symptoms or pain with sex can affect daily life and intimacy. You can name the specific symptom even if discussing it feels awkward. Ask whether an examination is appropriate and what nonprescription and prescription options may fit you. The answer depends on the cause, your health history and your preferences.

A product sold as a general menopause remedy may not address the symptom that bothers you. Check the purpose and instructions, and tell your clinician if a product causes irritation. You do not have to accept painful symptoms as an unavoidable part of aging.

### If cost or access is a barrier

Tell your care team if an option is hard to afford, difficult to obtain, or incompatible with your schedule. Ask about alternatives, insurance coverage and follow-up by phone or video where available. A plan that works on paper may need adjustment to work in daily life.

## Preparing for a useful follow-up

Before your visit, note which symptoms are most disruptive, when they started and how they affect sleep, work or relationships. Bring a list of medications and supplements, including products marketed for menopause. If you have questions about family or personal health history, write down what you know; it is fine not to have every answer.

At the end of the visit, ask what you and your clinician agreed to try, when you should notice a change, when to check in and which side effects need an earlier call. Ask for written instructions if that helps. You can bring a trusted person or request another conversation if you need more time to weigh the options.

## FAQ

### Is hormone therapy safe for everyone?

No treatment suits everyone. A clinician should weigh your symptoms and personal risks with you. ACOG lists conditions for which systemic hormone therapy is usually not recommended, including a history of blood clots, stroke, heart attack or certain cancers.

### Do I need progesterone if I take estrogen?

If you still have a uterus, systemic estrogen is generally paired with a progestin to protect the uterine lining. After a hysterectomy, estrogen alone may be an option. Ask which type applies to you.

### What can I try if I do not want hormones?

Ask about evidence-based nonhormonal prescription medicines and approaches for the symptoms that bother you. The best choice depends on your health history and goals.

### Does black cohosh work for hot flashes?

ACOG does not recommend plant and herbal remedies such as black cohosh for hot flashes. Discuss any supplement you are considering with your clinician.

### Do I have to wait until my periods stop to ask for help?

No. Symptoms can begin during perimenopause. A clinician can discuss your symptoms and whether another cause should be considered.

### Can I use a supplement with a prescription?

Ask a clinician or pharmacist first. Supplements can interact with medicines and their safety and effectiveness vary. Bring the actual bottle so the ingredients can be reviewed.
